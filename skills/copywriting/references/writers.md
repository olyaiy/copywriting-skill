# Writer by writer

Signature advice from 38 of the book's 53 contributors, in the book's alphabetical
order. Use this when someone asks for a particular writer's approach, or when you
want a different way into a brief. Wording is paraphrased; short phrases in quotation
marks are as reported by the sources listed in the repository README.

| Writer | Signature advice |
| --- | --- |
| David Abbott (AMV; The Economist, Volvo) | Dig first, then write. Use your own life to animate copy. Think visually. Make lists not read like lists. Confess. Do not be boring. |
| Nick Asbury (2018 edition) | Writing has changed since 1995, so understand the context you write in. His reading of the whole book: it is all about seeing from the reader's side. |
| John Bevins | If you do not enjoy writing it, no one will enjoy reading it. Repay borrowed attention. Great ads write themselves once you dig up the gem. |
| Mike Boles | Do not be afraid to use no words. |
| Tony Brignull (CDP; Parker, Army) | Treat the company as a person. Run the drinks-party test. One word and a thousand facts. Beware adjectives. |
| Eugene Cheong | His printed drafts show lines crossed out and rewritten. The first take is rarely the last. |
| Marty Cooke | Good writing is good thinking. Get away from the screen and read outside your field. |
| Tony Cox | The intro hooks the reader or nothing does. |
| Tim Delaney | Get to the point. Never pad. Repetition weakens copy. |
| Simon Dicketts | Find an attitude and a tone, then hold them. Develop your own style. Write what you will be proud of years later. |
| Sean Doyle | Volume of ideas forces originality. Always be collecting. Arrive early. Avoid brainstorms. Pick a side. Less is not always more. |
| Malcolm Duffy | Do not write, rewrite. Aim for surprise. Read constantly. Is it worth memorising? |
| Paul Fishlock | You are in the influence business, not entertainment. Ground creativity in the science of persuasion. Watch real buyers. |
| Richard Foster | Keep a small editor in your head shouting at you to get to the point. |
| Neil French | Never adopt the brand's voice. Write as yourself, a salesman speaking for the client. Long copy works when it is interesting. |
| Dan Germain (Innocent; 2018 edition) | Just start. Write on the move. Remember it is not that serious. |
| Malcolm Gluck | Remove every line the copy survives without. Read aloud. Let copy settle for days before showing anyone. |
| Steve Harrison | "You" should outnumber "I" and "we" three to one. |
| Steve Hayden (Apple "1984") | Please the reader, not the client or yourself. The first line of copy may matter more than the headline. Bring out the client's best self. |
| Steve Henry (HHCL) | Body copy is barely read. Be entertaining and do it differently. The next great copy will look like nothing in the book. |
| Susie Henry | Weave selling points into one empathetic argument and make it look easy. Work in isolation. There is no formula. |
| Adrian Holmes | Ten tips: use the deadline, decide the ending first, reward the reader, do not over-season, read poetry, read aloud, do not be precious, treat copy as a visual object, use sonata form, tear it up and do it better. |
| Leon Jaume (WCRS) | You are a gatecrasher. Choose a role to find a voice. Tell a story. Write fast. You need not end on a pun. |
| Eric Kallman (2018 edition) | "Seinfeld it" and "Will Ferrell it": two observation passes on the product, then combine them. |
| Janet Kestin (2018 edition) | Get inside the skin of the person you write for. A demographic profile is not a person. |
| Bob Levenson (DDB; Volkswagen) | "Dear Charlie". Audience categories are worse than useless. The prospect is smarter and warier than you. |
| Andy McLeod | A great headline is the truth about the product, told arrestingly. Smart writing is simple writing. |
| Barbara Nokes | "Empathy really is all." |
| Tim Riley | The best copy can be no copy. Working where writing is not prized is good discipline. |
| Nigel Roberts | Art direction catches the eye, copy does the talking. Publicity informs, advertising sells. |
| Andrew Rutherford | Eight steps: attention, intrigue, target, superiority, facts, desire, credibility, close. |
| John Salmon | Write from the reader's self-interest. |
| Indra Sinha (Amnesty) | At its deepest level it is all about the reader. Trust your heart. |
| John Stingley | Writing ads is acting: become the audience first. Be a student of humanity. |
| Tom Thomas | Being believed is harder than being noticed. State claims as facts. Assume the reader is as bright as you. |
| Dave Trott | Only about 5% of 5% read the copy, so ask who it is for. |
| Mary Wear | Know the target intuitively. Know when to stop. Charm, do not corner. |
| Dan Wieden (Wieden+Kennedy) | Most advice on ad writing is of little value. Write something startling or write nothing. |

## Notes on reliability

- Not summarised here, because no reliable public account of their essays was found:
  Tony Barry, Jim Durfee, Lionel Hunt, Mike Lescarbeau, James Lowther, Alfredo
  Marcantonio, Ed McCabe, Tim Mellors, Chris O'Shea, Jagdish Ramakrishnan, Paul
  Silverman, Steve Simpson, Jeremy Sinclair and Luke Sullivan.
- Some of the Brignull points come from masterclasses he gave after the book, and
  Sinha's "trust your heart" comes from a separate talk about his Amnesty ads.
- The Gluck entry rests on an extract printed in Campaign whose byline position is
  ambiguous.
- "No words" is credited to Mike Boles by one reader of the book and to John Bevins
  by another.
- Do not imitate a living writer's voice so closely that copy could be mistaken for
  theirs. Use the approach, not the signature.
