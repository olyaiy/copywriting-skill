# The twelve recurring principles

The Copy Book has no shared method. Its 53 writers work differently and often
contradict each other. These twelve ideas are the ones that recur across the essays
whatever the writer's style. Names in brackets are the writers each idea is
associated with. Wording is paraphrased.

## 1. Dig first, then write

Do not start until you have more to say than you can use. Fluent copy is impossible
if research keeps interrupting the writing.

- David Abbott spends most of his time fact-finding and will not begin until he has
  too much material.
- Tony Brignull's phrase is "one word and a thousand facts". Ask what, where, why
  and how. Interview the client in person.
- John Bevins says great ads write themselves once you find the gem, so dig where
  others would not look.
- Paul Fishlock watches real buyers at the moment of decision, and reads psychology
  and persuasion research. Creativity grounded in proven principles beats creativity
  flying blind.

## 2. Write to one person, not a demographic

- The book's preface (Anthony Simonds-Gooding) tells you to picture a single typical
  customer, perhaps someone you know, and get inside their head and heart.
- Bob Levenson calls categories like "males, 18 to 34" worse than useless. He wrote
  every ad as a letter beginning "Dear Charlie", then crossed the greeting out.
- Janet Kestin: a profile of age, household and income is not a person.
- John Stingley compares it to acting. Drop your own identity and take on the
  audience's interests, fears, tastes and biases.
- Mary Wear: know the reader intuitively, not just intellectually.
- Barbara Nokes: "Empathy really is all."
- Steve Hayden: please the reader, not the client and not yourself.

## 3. Find the truth before the words

- Andy McLeod: a great headline is the truth about the product told in an arresting
  way. A good line is a clear, concise expression of a clever idea, not clever
  writing. Take what people assume about the brand and apply the truth to it.
- Marty Cooke: good writing is good thinking. The thinking happens away from the
  screen.
- Simon Dicketts: find an attitude, not a list of facts.

## 4. You are an uninvited guest

- Adrian Holmes: every piece of copy is a deal. The reader continues only while you
  keep them interested.
- John Bevins: when you borrow a stranger's attention, give something valuable back.
- Leon Jaume: copywriters are gatecrashers. Study how newspaper columnists earn
  their readers.
- Tom Thomas and Bob Levenson: assume the reader is at least as bright as you, and
  more wary, because they do not work in advertising.

## 5. Facts persuade more than claims

- Tom Thomas: the hard part is not being noticed, it is being believed. Facts are
  more believable than claims, so state claims as facts.
- David Abbott: if facts persuade, learn to write a list that does not read like a
  list.
- Andrew Rutherford: facts beat empty claims, and a little humour helps them go down.

## 6. Sound like a person

- Tony Brignull's copy improved when he saw that a company can be related to as a
  person. His test: would you say these words to a stranger at a drinks party? If
  she is engaged, carry on. If she reaches for the peanuts, start again.
- David Abbott owns no thesaurus. He wants plain, simple, familiar words and sees
  himself as a paid advocate.
- Steve Harrison: "you" should outnumber "I" and "we" about three to one.

## 7. Think visually

- David Abbott: never write what a picture says better. Sometimes the headline is
  really a brief for the designer. He draws the shape of the ad and writes the line
  inside its borders.
- Mike Boles: do not be afraid to use no words.
- Tim Riley, recalling John Hegarty: the best copy can be no copy.
- Nigel Roberts: art direction catches the eye, copy does the talking.

## 8. Do not be boring

- David Abbott lists it as one of five things he believes are more or less true.
- Malcolm Duffy: if your words do not surprise you, they will not surprise anyone.
- Neil French: nobody reads boring long copy, but people do read interesting long
  copy. Length is not the problem.
- Dan Wieden: if you cannot write something startling, do not write anything.

## 9. Rewrite without mercy

- Malcolm Duffy: do not write, rewrite. Ask whether the copy is worth memorising.
- Adrian Holmes: the good is the enemy of the great. Tear it up and do it again,
  better. Do not be precious about your wording.
- Richard Foster keeps a small editor in his head shouting at him to get to the
  point.
- Malcolm Gluck: remove any line the copy survives without. Read it aloud. Do not
  show it until days have passed without a significant change.
- Tony Brignull: pin it to the wall and show nobody until the next morning.

## 10. Admit something

- David Abbott, citing Bill Bernbach: a small admission gains a large acceptance.
  Confession is good for copy.

## 11. The job is to sell

- Paul Fishlock: you are in the influence business, not the entertainment business.
  The reader should think "what a great product", not "what a great ad".
- Nigel Roberts: publicity informs, advertising sells.
- Andrew Rutherford's sequence ends with closing the sale: make the buyer want to
  act, then make them act.

## 12. There is no formula

- Susie Henry: there is no magic formula. The trick is to weave selling points into
  one persuasive argument and make it look easy.
- Steve Henry: the next great piece of copy will look like nothing in the book,
  because those ideas have been done.
- Dan Wieden: most personal advice on ad writing is of little value.
